01

Why this matters now

Interest in AI is moving faster than governance. Employees are already experimenting with AI tools on their own, whether or not there is a policy for it.

96%
of surveyed mayors are interested in using generative AI
38%
of local government tech leaders say their organization isn't prepared to use AI safely
9%
of local governments have a formal internal AI policy

Sources: Johns Hopkins / Bloomberg Center for Government Excellence (2023) · ICMA · Public Technology Institute (2024), as cited by NLC

The gap between adoption and governance is where most of the risk, confusion and opportunity lives.

"Should we allow AI?" is the wrong question. The better question is "How should we use it?" AI is already entering our organizations through employees, vendors, software and public expectations. A ban doesn't stop that; it pushes use out of sight, into personal accounts and unapproved tools. The useful work is in the middle, between "ban everything" and "anything goes."

02

Know the tool

"We use ChatGPT" or "we use Claude" doesn't tell you much. Think of AI like a vehicle: two people can "use the same AI" while actually using very different systems.

Platform
The vehicle
ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot
Model
The engine
GPT, Claude Sonnet / Opus, Gemini models
Tools
What it can access or do
Web search, file access, connected apps
Data
The cargo you put inside
Whatever you paste, upload or connect

03

Know the edges

Ask four questions before any AI-assisted work leaves your desk.

Data
What did you give it?
Accuracy
Is the output true?
Decision
What authority did you give it?
Accountability
Who owns the result?

AI confidence is not AI accuracy. AI can be spectacularly helpful and spectacularly wrong with exactly the same confidence. A polished, well-formatted answer is not the same thing as a correct one; the interface doesn't look nervous when it's guessing.

A good answer can look like "I don't know." When AI lacks what it needs, the most responsible response is often: "I can't determine this from the information provided." Evaluate AI not only on whether it answers, but on whether it knows when not to.

04

Build the guardrails

Govern without banning. A guardrail says "use AI here, under these conditions." A roadblock just says "don't."

GreenNormal work
Brainstorming, drafting, formatting, public information, general research
Use it like any other tool.
YellowSlow down
Internal documents, data analysis, HR work, claims or incident information, legal research
Use an approved tool, and a human checks it before it goes anywhere.
RedStop or reframe
Credentials, unauthorized sensitive information, autonomous high-impact decisions
Change the task, not just the tool.

Yellow does not mean no. Yellow means slow down enough to use the tool responsibly.

If you remember nothing else

The Four-Question Guardrail

Ask these before you hit submit.

  1. DataWhat am I giving it? Would I be comfortable if this leaked?
  2. DecisionWhat am I asking it to do? Assist, or actually decide?
  3. EnvironmentWhere is the information going? An approved tool, or a personal account?
  4. Double-checkWhat must a human verify before this goes out?
05

Take it home

A policy document can't answer questions your organization has never discussed. Work through these six questions with your leadership team before you write one.

Closing

Know the tool. Know the edges. Build the guardrails.

AI can organize, summarize, compare and surface patterns. Humans still judge, approve, interpret, decide and own the result. To make that work, give your people approved tools, clear boundaries, human accountability and permission to experiment.

None of this requires you to be an AI expert. It requires you to be clear about where the line is.

Good guardrails don't prevent experimentation; they make responsible experimentation possible.
